Air quality drift watch

rebuilt weekly

Models go stale. The world they were trained on moves on, and usually nobody notices until something downstream breaks. This system notices on its own, every week, with nobody watching it. The job it does is forecasting how dirty a city's air will be (PM2.5, the soot fine enough to reach your bloodstream) a week ahead, using nothing but the weather forecast. When the model starts slipping, the system trains a replacement, then refuses to ship it unless it beats the one already in service. Six cities, running since 2025.

One city at a time

Six cities, each with its own model and its own history of retraining. Every setting is identical across all of them, so where two cities behave differently it is their air that differs and not their tuning. Pick one, or see all six side by side.

What holds across all six

Nothing below this line depends on which city is selected. These are the findings about the loop itself, and each one needs more than a single city to make. The all-cities view puts the six on shared axes.

Why six models and not one?

Each city here gets its own model, retrained on its own schedule. The obvious cheaper arrangement is one model over all six at once, so the benchmark on every city carries it as a predictor: one Ridge, trained once on all six training windows, never retrained.

A seven-day exam certifies a model for a month

Every promotion on this page was decided by one exam: a week of air neither model had seen, with the challenger needing to win by more than 5%. That margin is the number the decision was made on, so it cannot also be the evidence the decision was right. That would be marking your own homework. The check is what each winner went on to do over the weeks it served, measured against the model it displaced and scored on those same weeks.

Whether the detection works at all

When an alarm goes off in a real city, there is no way to know whether it was right. Nobody knows what the true answer was supposed to be, so an alarm firing at random would look much the same as one that worked. So the same system is pointed at a made-up world with two dials. One changes the weather. The other changes how the weather turns into pollution. Here the right answer is known in advance, because we set it. Each alarm should react to its own dial and stay silent for the other. That is what the charts below test.

How this works

The loop

One scheduled run, every week. Steps 3 and 4 only happen when step 2 says they should.

    Why two alarms and not one: the first looks only at the weather coming in, so it can fire immediately, without waiting to find out whether the model was wrong. But “the world looks different” is not the same as “the model is failing”. Kraków shows the gap. Through the summer its weather drifts further from training than anywhere else on this page, while the model quietly gets better. So the cheap alarm watches, and only the expensive one, the one asking whether we got this wrong, can authorise spending money on a retrain.

    What this page shows is wrong with it: the expensive alarm compares the model against its own error at training time, and every promotion resets that comparison. Because retrains fire in the dirty season, each new model inherits a higher bar than the one it replaced, and the bar never comes back down. You can see it as a staircase in “when it retrains, and against what bar”. Once it has ratcheted to the seasonal peak the alarm cannot fire again, whatever the model does. Kraków spends its last 30 weeks that way, serving a 210-day-old model while the trigger reports it as healthier than it has ever been.

    And what happened when it was fixed: two changes were proposed, and building them settled the question differently. An absolute error floor turns out to be impossible: waking the quietest city needs a floor low enough to retrain the dirtiest one every week, and there is nothing in between. A yardstick that holds still when a model is promoted is buildable, and the loop now carries it. It also fires when skill against the daily profile drops below a floor, which wakes the alarm up as intended, taking Kraków's longest silence from 30 weeks down to 5. Replayed across all six cities it then changes nothing in five of the six at a cautious setting, and at an aggressive one makes two of them worse for one improvement. So it ships switched off. The alarm going quiet was never what cost anything. The seven-day exam below is, and that is knowable only because the fix was measured rather than assumed.

    The same loop, running on a schedule

    The model

    It answers one question: given the weather we expect in a week, how dirty will the air be? It is fed the weather forecasts as they were published at the time, rather than the weather that turned out to happen, so it starts a step behind in the same way anyone forecasting a week out really would. It is never told how dirty the air has been recently, which would be an easy shortcut and would hide the effect being demonstrated. Keeping it simple is a choice: a simple model visibly falls apart when the world shifts, where a clever one papers over the cracks and you learn nothing. What is worth looking at is the machinery around the model. Swap in something bigger and none of that machinery changes.
